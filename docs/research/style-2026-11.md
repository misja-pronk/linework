# A signature style for Linework

November 2026. Question: a look that is distinct, professional, and that people want to use. The bar: you can
tell at a glance it is a Linework diagram, and a consultant pastes it into a customer deck or a board
document without touching it.

Three directions were designed and drawn on the same four examples (Azure hub and spoke with nested groups,
an ERD, a sequence diagram, data products), each over several rounds of looking at the PNGs and changing them.
The competitor overview is in `market-2026-11.md` and is not repeated here.

How the prototypes were made, so the pictures are read correctly:

- Folders without `-x` in the name were drawn by the shipping renderer from a `*.theme.yaml` file only. That
  is what a theme can do today.
- Folders with `-x` were drawn by a patched copy of the renderer (a snapshot of HEAD 18a18e5 in the scratch
  folder, never the repo) that reads experimental tokens. Each patch is one of the renderer changes listed
  below. Nothing in the repo was changed.
- Scratch folder, called `$S` below:

  Theme files are in `$S/themes`, candidate tokens in `$S/candidates.mts`, the renderer patches in
  `$S/fork/make.py`, pictures in `$S/out`.

## 1. Recommendation: Survey

**The diagram as a map sheet.** White page, white cards. Groups are terrain: warm paper tints that get one
step deeper with every level of nesting, bounded by one hairline contour colour. Group names are small
letterspaced capitals, like region names on a map. Words on a line sit on the line in monospace with no box
around them, like a road number. Icons are drawn bare in one of ten map inks, without a coloured tile behind
them. A ruled KEY row closes the sheet, and four small corner marks show its extent. No shadows, no
gradients, no ornament.

### Why this one

1. **Its signature does a job.** The depth tints are the thing you recognise, and they are also what makes
   nesting readable: subscription, network, subnet are three visibly different grounds, and the white cards
   stand out on all of them. The current default gives every group the same wash, so nesting is carried by
   borders alone. A signature that costs legibility is what people reject in Excalidraw's hand font; one that
   adds legibility has no such complaint waiting for it.
2. **It matches what people praise.** Light background, restrained colour that carries meaning, one type
   system, labelled lines, real icons at one size, a legend. That is the common ground of the looks people
   ask "what tool made this?" about (Stripe, Cloudflare, Tailscale; section 2).
3. **It survives the customer deck.** The warm paper is quiet enough to sit next to any corporate palette.
   The cool grey version of the same design (`$S/out/survey-cool-r3-x/azure.png`) is just as tidy but looks
   like every SaaS dashboard; the warm paper is what makes it ours. That comparison decided it.
4. **It prints.** In greyscale the tints still step, lines still differ by dash, text stays dark
   (`$S/out/survey-r5-x-grey/data.png`).
5. **It is honest to the name.** Hypsometric tints, contour lines, a key and sheet marks are linework, not
   decoration borrowed from somewhere else. Only the conventions that carry information were kept; compass
   roses, parchment and north arrows were left out on purpose (Ordnance Survey calls a needless north arrow a
   beginner's mistake).

### Why it beats the other two

- **Transit** is bolder and more recognisable from across a room, but it is loud: thick lines and filled
  roundels fight a customer's own slide design, and ERDs get heavy. Good as a choice, wrong as the default.
- **Drafting** is the most distinctive and prints perfectly, but it gives up colour, and non-technical
  readers see a wireframe or a terminal theme. D2 users report exactly that problem with stark themes
  ("a hard sell to orgs where management isn't technical").

### Risks

- **Most of it needs renderer work.** A theme file alone gets the palette, the font, flat cards and tighter
  corners, and the result reads as a quieter `slate` (`$S/out/survey-r5/azure.png`). The identity comes from
  depth tints, bare icons, on-line labels and caps group labels; those are renderer changes 1 to 4 below.
- **Colour is not lightness-coded.** The ten inks have about the same darkness on purpose (each passes
  4.5:1 on white), so in greyscale they look alike. The style therefore leans on the rule that colour is
  never the only carrier: icon shape, caption, line pattern. One real gap: a legend entry for a node
  category is a coloured dot, which is ambiguous in greyscale. Fix proposed below (legend swatch shows the
  kind's icon).
- **Card edges are low contrast.** White card on the lightest tint is 1.08:1, the card border about 1.3 to
  1.6:1. The same is true of the current default and of most modern diagram styles, and the card is
  identified by its icon and label, but it is under WCAG's 3:1 for graphics if you read the box edge as
  required. A `contrast: high` switch (border `#7C8791`) would close it.
- **Warm paper next to a cold brand.** On a slide with a blue-grey template the tints can look slightly
  yellow. The `survey-cool` tints stay available as a one-line variant.
- **Two bundled fonts** (about 51 kB + 20 kB as Latin woff2) instead of one, and text measuring that is
  built on Inter metrics (see renderer change 12).
- **Colour-blind safety was not simulated.** See section 5.

**Shape**

- Radii: card 6, group 8, chip 4, edge bends 8, pill 999. (`radius: 0.5` on today's scale.)
- Strokes: card 1, group 1, edge 1.3, icon 1.6.
- Shadows: none (`shadow: 0`).
- Spacing: unchanged from the default tokens.

**Edges.** Orthogonal with rounded bends. Solid, dashed `6 4`, dotted `1.5 3.5` as today. The filled
arrowhead with the concave back and the crow's-foot markers stay as they are. Two additions: where an
upright line crosses another line it bridges it (a 4 px gap in the ground colour), and where lines join the
junction is a small ring (radius 3.4, card-colour fill, line-colour stroke) instead of a filled dot.

**Groups.** Fill is the terrain tint for the group's depth; outline is the contour colour, 1 px, dashed
`4 3` when the model says dashed. Header: a 14 px icon in the group's tone, the label in letterspaced
capitals, then the caption. The tone of a group now shows only in its icon; the fill no longer changes with
tone.

**Edge labels.** No pill. A borderless patch in the colour of the ground under the label (card white or the
tint of the group it lies in) breaks the line, and the words sit in the gap in mono.

**Icons.** Bare: 22 px, stroke 1.6 in the tone's `fg`, no tile. (`tile: bare`.) Vendor icon packs keep their
own colours and sit in the same 34 px slot.

**Chips and badges.** Unchanged in construction: `bg` fill, radius 4, 11 px icon and 11 / 500 text in `fg`.

**Tables (ERD).** Header in terrain tint 2 with a contour rule under it, key and link icons in ink colours,
type column right-aligned in mono.

**Sequence.** Participant boxes use the terrain tint and contour, their labels are capitals like group
labels, header icons are bare, step numbers stay solid ink circles with white numerals (the convention AWS
also prescribes), and the halo behind a message label takes the colour of the ground it lies on.

**Legend.** A hairline rule across the full width, then one row: the word KEY in small capitals, then the
swatches. Lines are drawn as line samples in their own dash; node categories as a dot in the tone's ink.

**Title block and sheet marks.** Title and subtitle top left as today. Four L-shaped corner marks (9 px
arms, 1 px, faint) at 12 px from the edge show the sheet's extent, so a white diagram does not float on a
white slide and needs no box. With a `titleBlock`, the optional framed sheet adds a 1 px ink neatline, a
13 px zone band (numbers along the top and bottom, letters down the sides, about every 260 px, in mono) and
an imprint at the right end of the KEY row built from client, project, version, date and classification.
The imprint only shows fields the author gave; it never invents a date, so output stays reproducible.

**Background.** Plain white (dark: `#13171B`). No grid, no paper texture.

**Rules that keep it recognisable.** Tints mean nesting and nothing else. One accent per diagram. Tone is
never the only carrier. Nothing gets a shadow. Capitals only for group names and the KEY.

## 2. Evidence

Quotes from Hacker News were read verbatim through the HN API (`news.ycombinator.com/item?id=…`). Other
pages were read through a page-reading tool, so treat their wording as close, not exact. Reddit and X could
not be reached.

### Which looks people praise, share and copy

- **A house style that is one system.** Stripe's diagrams: "very well made and the branding is consistent
  and beautiful" (HN 25459064); they are built in code from "a set of composable elements" that "can be
  restyled universally" (stripe.dev/blog/markdoc). Cloudflare's designers describe diagrams that are
  "simplified and have a clear flow from left to right", on "light or white backgrounds", with "consistent
  visual terminology". Tailscale's get the question every tool wants: "is there any good tool that creates
  charts like these?" (31271594), answered with "A designer drew them" (31272940).
- **Bold, short, readable.** Whimsical: "Well-designed, beautiful, and ergonomic." (38036765), "It is bold
  and easy to read." (27166538). IcePanel: "this looks pretty polished" (34343139).
- **A recognisable device.** Rough.js is "the library that gives Excalidraw its unique look"; a Mermaid
  diagram converted to Excalidraw "just looks like a mermaid diagram then and not an excalidraw" (47572094).
  So the look lives in a few devices, and people notice when they are missing.

### What people reject

- **Handwriting in formal work.** "It also looks very unprofessional so if you ever have to write
  customer-facing diagrams, you have tool fragmentation." (47577014); "the default font is ugly, childish
  and inaccessible" (47572014); "My dyslexia makes it really difficult for me to read it." (29116526);
  "the style takes away from the actual content" (47573453).
- **Dated or programmer-looking defaults.** Mermaid: "quite ugly by default" (47831194), "wished they looked
  more professional" (46805242). PlantUML: wanted something that "looked like it wasn't drawn in MS Paint in
  1996" (35745548). D2: "the default theme (and other ones) is a hard sell to orgs where management isn't
  technical" (45713782); "the spacing/padding, colors, font, and font size they're using are ridiculous"
  (38037037).
- **Effects.** Cloudcraft's isometric view: "I'm not a fan of the fancy 3d visualization." (10723457). D2
  itself rejected hatch fills for its sketch mode.
- **No key.** C4: "Every diagram should have a key/legend"; the common faults are "unexplained symbols,
  ambiguous relationships between elements, no key/legend" (21034069).

### Why sketchy looks are liked, and what that means for us

People like the sketch look because it lowers the stakes: "the sketchy look makes it clear designs are
rough and not blueprints" (47571719); "make the whole thing a bit shabby, and the need for perfection
disappears." (29116069). The other side: "if something looks too polished people assume that polish is all
the way" (38043791), and "Do you want to scream professionalism, authority, and completed? Use a regular
UML tool." (47572728). Linework's diagrams are generated from a model and kept in git, so they are the
finished kind. The default should say "finished". A draft mode is a separate question (a `draft` watermark
in the imprint would be the Survey way to say it).

### What makes a style read as professional

Drawn from the vendor rules and the opinions above (the summary is my inference; the parts are sourced):

- One type family, few sizes. AWS: "All label text should be 12pt Arial".
- Straight lines and right angles. AWS: "Use straight lines and right angles to connect objects wherever
  possible." Beck and Vignelli kept lines to horizontal, vertical and 45 degrees.
- Colour means something. "colors should convey meaning rather than being aesthetic or artistic choices"
  (38039163). Consulting slide guides limit a palette to three or four colours (secondhand source).
- Flat. Google Cloud's deck: "Zones do not have shadows". NN/g's flat-design research warns only where flat
  hides what is clickable, which a static diagram does not have.
- Icons untouched and at one size. Azure: "Don't crop, flip, or rotate icons".
- A legend, single-direction arrows, layers instead of everything at once. Azure: "Provide a legend",
  "Avoid bidirectional arrows", "Layer, don't overload", "consistently pair color with pattern".
- Restraint. Linear describes its redesign as "reduce visual noise… more neutral and timeless appearance";
  Tufte's chartjunk argument is the older form of the same rule.

What reads as dated or toy: gradients and drop shadows, 3D, saturated rainbow fills, hand fonts, clip-art
icons at mixed sizes, thick black borders on everything, unlabelled lines.

### Fonts and colour systems: licences

- **SIL OFL 1.1** allows "to use, study, copy, merge, embed, modify, redistribute, and sell", bundling "with
  any software, provided that each copy contains the above copyright notice and this license", and says
  "The requirement for fonts to remain under this license does not apply to any document created using the
  Font Software." So an MIT tool can ship OFL fonts with their licence files, and exported diagrams carry no
  obligation. Embedding a font in an SVG as base64 is covered by "embed" (my reading). Subsetting a font
  that has a Reserved Font Name may count as a modified version that must be renamed; check per font before
  shipping subsets.
- OFL 1.1, licence text read in the google/fonts repo: Inter (no reserved name upstream), Instrument Sans,
  Geist, Geist Mono, IBM Plex Sans / Mono / Sans Condensed (reserved name "Plex"), Schibsted Grotesk,
  Hanken Grotesk, Barlow, JetBrains Mono, Public Sans, Figtree, Manrope, DM Sans, Archivo, Source Serif 4,
  Newsreader, Fraunces, Overpass, Red Hat Text, Atkinson Hyperlegible.
- Tabular figures (measured on the Google Fonts builds): Inter, Geist, Instrument Sans, Schibsted Grotesk,
  Barlow have `tnum`; IBM Plex Sans and Hanken Grotesk are tabular by default; DM Sans, Fraunces, Libre
  Franklin and Instrument Serif have none.
- Not usable: osifont (GPL), Bahnschrift (proprietary), any DIN or Helvetica cut.
- Colour systems: Radix Colors (MIT), Open Color (MIT), Tailwind palette (MIT), IBM Carbon (Apache 2.0),
  ColorBrewer (Apache 2.0), d3-scale-chromatic incl. Tableau 10 and Observable 10 (ISC), Paul Tol's schemes
  (his Python file is BSD 3-clause), Okabe-Ito (widely reused, no licence stated). The Survey inks are our
  own values, checked for contrast; they are not copied from any of these.

### Accessibility

- WCAG 1.4.3: text needs "a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1" (large text 3:1). 1.4.11: graphics needed to
  understand the content need "at least 3:1 against adjacent color(s)". 1.4.1: "Color is not used as the
  only visual means of conveying information".
- Okabe and Ito: "Show difference BOTH in color and shape". Datawrapper: "You can use any colors you like as
  long as they vary by lightness." Only Paul Tol's three-colour high-contrast scheme is documented as safe
  for both colour-blind readers and greyscale print; ColorBrewer marks no qualitative set as photocopy-safe
  beyond three classes. So no ten-colour set can be the only carrier, and Survey does not try: tone always
  travels with an icon, a caption or a line pattern.
- Measured for Survey (script `$S/contrast.mjs`): see the contrast columns in section 1. The theme system
  already repairs any tone `fg` below 4.5:1 on its tile. It checks `faint` only at 3:1 on the canvas; with
  tinted groups the check should run against the deepest tint, or captions on tints should use `muted` (as
  the spec does).

## 3. The other two candidates

Both hold up when drawn and deserve to ship as optional built-in themes. Suggested set of built-ins after
this work: `survey` (default; `light` stays as an alias of the old look for a release), `survey-dark`
(replaces `dark`), `transit`, `drafting` (replaces `mono`), and `slate` / `ocean` kept as plain brand
examples.

### Transit

**The lines are the diagram.** Thick routes in strong colours with 45 degree bends, round "station" icons
(a filled roundel with a white glyph), ring interchanges where routes join, flat neutral grey zones with no
outline, route names in capitals in the colour of their line. After Beck and Vignelli.

Pictures: `$S/out/transit-r4-x/azure.png`, `$S/out/transit-r4-x/data.png`, all four in
`$S/out/transit-r4-sheet.png`. Round 1 (theme file only, `$S/out/transit-r1/azure.png`) was heavy and lost
the nesting; depth-stepped greys, a lighter card edge, roundels and scaled dashes fixed that.

Where it is strong: flows and pipelines, slides shown on a projector, anything where the routes are the
story. Where it is weak: ERDs (2.3 px crow's feet are heavy), dense diagrams (many roundels compete), decks
with a strong brand colour of their own. Line-coloured labels on the deepest zone reach only 4.0:1 for teal;
on zones deeper than the first they should fall back to ink.

### Drafting

**An engineering drawing.** Ink lines on white, square corners, one signal colour, monospace for every
literal, labels on small plates, a faint dot grid, and a drawing frame with zone references and a title
imprint (after ISO 5457 and ISO 7200). No fills at all.

Pictures: `$S/out/drafting-r4-x/azure.png`, `$S/out/drafting-r4-x/data.png`, all four in
`$S/out/drafting-r4-sheet.png`. Round 1 set the whole diagram in IBM Plex Mono
(`$S/out/drafting-r1/azure.png`): it looked like a terminal theme, and text overflowed because widths are
measured with Inter metrics. Moving prose to IBM Plex Sans and keeping mono for literals fixed both.

Where it is strong: audit and compliance documents, printed handouts, greyscale, technical readers. Where
it is weak: it gives up tone colour, so legend categories are told apart by text only (the swatches become
identical outline squares); non-technical readers may see a wireframe.

## 4. Implementation list

### Theme tokens only (works with today's theme files)

Ordered by visual impact. The files exist: `$S/themes/survey.theme.yaml`, `survey-dark.theme.yaml`,
`transit.theme.yaml`, `drafting.theme.yaml`. Result for Survey: `$S/out/survey-r5/`.

1. Palette: white canvas and cards, ink text, warm `wash` and one `line` colour on every tone, `harmonize:
   false` so the values are used as written.
2. `shadow: 0`.
3. `font`: Instrument Sans with three weight files (Transit: Barlow; Drafting: IBM Plex Sans).
4. `radius: 0.5` (Transit 1.4, Drafting 0.15) and `lineWeight: 0.93`.
5. Eight tones with `fg` values that already pass 4.5:1, so no warnings.
6. `legend: bottom`; `tile: soft` (Transit `solid`, Drafting `outline`).

What this cannot do: a tint per nesting depth (a wash belongs to a tone, so nested groups of one tone look
the same), anything about icons beyond the three tile styles, labels on lines, capitals, a second font, the
KEY row, sheet marks.

### Renderer changes needed

Ordered by visual impact for Survey. Each one is implemented in the prototype
(`$S/fork/make.py`, as a list of small replacements against `packages/render/src/svg.ts` and
`sequence.ts`), so the patch shows the size of the change. Token names are proposals; in the prototype they
live under `theme.x`.

| # | Change | Proposed token | Used by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Group fill by nesting depth; one outline colour for all groups. The edge-label and line-bridge code needs the same lookup ("what ground is under this point") | `group.tints: [hex, hex, hex]`, `group.line: hex` | all three |
| 2 | Icon treatments without a soft tile; size of the bare glyph | `tile: bare \| disc \| ring` (next to soft, solid, outline), `iconSize` | Survey bare, Transit disc |
| 3 | Edge label styles: borderless patch in the ground colour, or a bordered plate; ink from the text or from the line; capitals | `edgeLabel: pill \| halo \| plate`, `edgeLabelInk: muted \| text \| line`, `edgeLabelCaps` | all three |
| 4 | A second font family with roles | `font.mono: { family, files }`, `mono: [edgeLabel, type]` | Survey, Drafting |
| 5 | Group header: capitals with tracking, label and caption colour, icon size or none | `group.label: caps`, `group.labelSize`, `group.tracking`, `group.labelColor`, `group.captionColor`, `group.icon` | all three |
| 6 | Legend as a KEY row: rule across, heading, then swatches; a category swatch follows the icon treatment (dot, roundel, outline square). Better still: draw the kind's icon as the swatch, which also works in greyscale (not prototyped) | `legendStyle: key` | all three |
| 7 | Sheet marks: corner marks; or neatline, centring ticks and zone grid. With a frame the page padding must grow by the band width (in the prototype the title sits close to the frame) | `sheet: { marks: corners \| frame, inset, zones, ticks }` | Survey, Drafting |

| 11 | Sequence diagrams follow the theme: boxes use group tints and caps labels, header icons use the tile style, the label halo takes the ground colour instead of always the canvas, step numbers can be rings | `number: solid \| ring` | all three |
| 12 | Text measuring per font. Widths are measured with Inter metrics times one ratio; that breaks for monospace and for a second family (overflow seen in Drafting round 1 and with mono group captions) | none; measure with the face's own advance widths | needed for 4 |

## 5. Not verified

- Colour-blind simulation of the three palettes was not run; the claim rests on redundancy (icon, caption,
  dash), not on the colours.
- Whether Instrument Sans and Barlow declare a Reserved Font Name (matters only if we ship subsets).
- The OFL FAQ on subsetting and embedding was not read; the reading above is from the licence text.
- Dark palettes for Transit and Drafting are proposals and were not drawn.
- Survey was not tried on the other diagram types (kubernetes, network, access, github, c4) or with vendor
  icon packs, whose own colours may sit oddly on warm paper.
- Tufte's "Make all visual distinctions as subtle as possible, but still clear and effective" and the
  consulting three-to-four-colour rule come from secondary sources.
- ISO 5457 and ISO 7200 details come from summaries, not the standards.
- No user opinions were found on D2's Terminal and Origami themes, tldraw's look, Eraser, or Figma's and
  GitHub's blog diagrams.
- "Praised" and "rejected" rest mostly on Hacker News, which is a technical audience; no evidence was found
  from the consultants and board readers the bar is about.
